# Chapter 3: Electromagnetism

- **Electrostatics** is the study of electric charges that are stationary that create electric fields.
- **Magnetostatics** is the study of steady magnetic fields produced by electric currents that are constant in magnitude and direction. 

(See [[Electromagnetism variables and units]]).

## 1. Electric charges and fields

>[!abstract]- The elementary charge
> 
> The elementary charge $e$ is the magnitude of charge carried by a single proton ($+1e$) or electron ($-1e$). 
> 
> >[!abstract]+ Fundamental behaviors
> >
> >· Quantization: The charge must be an integer of $e$ ($q=ne$).
> >
> > · Conservation: The electric charge in a closed system remains constant over time (charge can be transferred but not created or destroyed).
> > 
> > · Additivity: The charges are scalar quantities that can be summed directly to find net charge of an object.
> >
> > · Forces of attraction/repulsion: Opposite charges attract and charges of the same sign repel each other.
> > 

The **transport of electric charges** represents an electric current, which is always connected with the transport of mass.

>[!example] Electrostatic force (Coulomb's law)
>
>$$\vec{F}=\frac{Q_1Q_2}{4\pi \epsilon_0 r^2}\hat{r}$$
> 
> If there are several charges $Q_i$ distributed in space, the total force is:
> 
> $$ \vec{F}= \frac q {4\pi \epsilon_0} \sum \frac{Q_i}{r_i^2}\hat{r_i}$$
> 

(Separation of electric charges)

>[!example] Electric field
>
>$$ \vec{E}(\vec{r})=\frac Q{4\pi \epsilon_0 r^2} \hat r $$

We can relate the force with the electric field with: $\large \vec{F} (\vec{r})= Q\vec{E}(\vec{r})$ .

The total charge $Q$ is the integral of all charge densities over the volume: $\large Q= \int_V \rho (\vec{r}) \; dV$ .

From here we rewrite the Coulomb force to get $\large \vec{F}(\vec{r})=\frac q {4\pi \epsilon_0} \int_V \frac{\vec{r}-\vec{r}'}{\left| \vec{r}-\vec{r}' \right| ^3} \rho (\vec{r}) \; dV$ .

>[!abstract]- Field lines
>
>The field can be illustrated by field lines:
>
> ![[Pasted image 20260712121014.png|300]]
> 
> The direction of the field strength is defined to show the direction a positive charge would experience from that field. The electric field lines always points from the positive to the negative charge or field, and they never intersect.
> 
> The magnitude or strength of the electric field is defined by the spacing and density of the field lines.

>[!example] Electric flux
>
>$$ \Phi_{el}=\int_S \vec{E} \; d\vec A= \int_V \vec{\nabla} ·\vec E \;dV$$ 
>
>Where we use **Gauss' theorem**: 
>
>$$\int_{\partial V} \vec F \;d\vec S=\int_V \vec{\nabla} · \vec F \; dV$$


## 2. Potential and Voltage, Electric multipoles

### Potentials


>[!abstract]- Work
>
>$$W=\int_{P_1}^{P_2} \vec F \; d\vec r= q \int_{P_1}^{P_2} \vec E \; d\vec r$$
>
>as the work that has to be done to bring a charge $q$ in an electric field $\vec E$ from the position $P_1$ to $P_2$.
>
>![[Screenshot 2026-07-12 123455.png|250]]

>[!example] Potential and Voltage
>
>Due to the electric field being conservative, we can define the **electric potential** at a point $P$:
>
>$$\phi (P)=\int_P^\infty \vec E \; d\vec r=\frac U q$$
>
> The zero is set at infinity ($\phi(r=\infty)=0$).
> 
> The electric potential energy (the work between two points with different potential), also called **electric voltage**, is defined as:
> 
> $$U=\phi (P_1) - \phi(P_2)=\int_{P_1}^{P_2} \vec E \; d\vec r$$
> 

Due to the conservation of energy, the kinetic energy must change by $\Delta E_{kin}=-\Delta E_{pot}$  with $\Delta E_{pot}=-qU$ .

>[!example] First equation of electrostatics
>
>$$\vec E=-\vec \nabla \phi$$
>
>
> $$\Rightarrow \vec\nabla \vec E=-\vec\nabla · \vec\nabla\phi=-\Delta \phi=\frac \rho {\epsilon_0}$$
>
>with $$\Delta \phi= -\frac \rho {\epsilon_0}$$ as the Poisson equation.

### Electric multipoles

We have:

$$ \phi (\vec r)= \frac 1 {4\pi \epsilon_0}\int_V \frac{\rho (\vec r')}{\left| \vec r - \vec r '\right|} dV$$

To obtain the **electric multipoles**, we do a Taylor's expansion to $\frac 1 {\left| \vec r - \vec r ' \right|}$ to obtain the **multipole expansion**, which divides the potential into sums $\phi_n(\vec r)$ that are generated by **monopoles**, **dipoles**, **quadrupoles**, and so on.

Electric **monopoles** and **dipoles** dominate the electric fields in matter because fundamental particles carry isolated net charges (monopoles) and the matter must be overall neutral (dipoles).
- If a system of charges has a net charge, the monopole term dominates entirely.
- If the net charge is zero, the dipole term becomes dominant.

Higher-order terms (**quadrupoles**, octupoles), drop off at faster rates and become negligible at all but the smallest subatomic distances.

>[!example]+ Electric dipole
>
> $$\phi_D(\vec r)=\frac 1 {4\pi\epsilon_0}·\frac {Q·\vec d·\vec r}{ r^3}=\frac 1 {4\pi\epsilon_0}·\frac {\vec p · \vec r}{r^3}$$
> 
> with the **dipole moment** $\vec p= Q· \vec d$ , with $\vec d$ as the distance from the negative charge to the positive charge.
> 
> ![[Screenshot 2026-07-12 133941.png|250]]

>[!example]+ Electric quadrupole
>
>$$\phi_Q(\vec r)= \frac Q{4\pi\epsilon_0}a · \vec\nabla (\frac{\vec d·\vec r}{r^3})=a·\vec\nabla \phi_D$$
>
>![[Screenshot 2026-07-12 134158.png|250]]

We then define the monopole, the dipole and the quadrupole as follows:

$$q_{\text{tot}} \equiv \sum_{i=1}^{N} q_i \qquad P_{\alpha} \equiv \sum_{i=1}^{N} q_i r_{i\alpha} \qquad Q_{\alpha\beta} \equiv \sum_{i=1}^{N} q_i \left( 3 r_{i\alpha} r_{i\beta} - \delta_{\alpha\beta} r_i^2 \right)$$

Then, the multipole expansion would be: 

$$ 4\pi\epsilon_0 V(\vec{r}) \equiv \sum_{i=1}^{N} q_i\, v(\vec{r}_i - \vec{r})= \frac{q_{\text{tot}}}{r}+ \frac{1}{r^3}\sum_{i=1}^3 P_{i} r_{i}+ \frac{1}{2r^5} \sum_{i,j = 1}^3 Q_{ij} r_{i} r_{j}+ \cdots$$


## 3. Conductors and dielectrics in electric fields

Inside a conductor there is no electric field. The charges are located only at the surface of the conductor as a reaction to the forces applied by the electric field.

![[Pasted image 20260712141143.png|250]]

### Capacitors

>[!abstract]+ Electric capacitance
>
>The electric capacitance is the ability of an object or system to store electrical charge.
>
>$$C=\frac Q U$$

For the **parallel plate capacitor**, we form a capacitor with two plates, at $x=0$ and $x=d$, with charges $Q$ and $-Q$.

![[Pasted image 20260712141904.png|300]]

The potential w.r.t. x is: $\large\phi(x)=-\frac U d x+\phi_0$ . 
Then, the capacitance: $C=\frac Q U =[\text{get Q from } E=\frac Q {A\epsilon_0}\text{ with } E=\frac U d]=\epsilon_0 \frac A d$.

>[!abstract]- Capacitors in parallel and in series
>
> In parallel, the voltage at each capacitor is the same : $$C=\sum_i C_i$$ . 
> 
> In series, the charges are separated by influence, so that the two neighboring plates have the same amount of charge but opposed sign. The voltage between two plates connected by a conductor is zero.

(Work and energy density with capacitance, diap. 11-15)

### Dielectrics

**Dielectrics** are electrical insulators that can be polarized by an applied electric field. The internal charges shift slightly when exposed to an electric field. Al matter made of atoms is dielectric.

The atomic displacement generates its own internal electric field that opposes the external field, creating tiny internal electric dipoles.

The capacitance for a dielectric is directly proportional to the capacitance in the vacuum by the dielectric constant (relative permittivity) $C_{diel}=\epsilon_r · C_{vac}$ , with $\epsilon_r>1$. The electric field is reduced by the dielectric constant $\vec E=\frac 1 {4\pi \epsilon_r \epsilon_0} \frac Q {r^2}\hat r$ . 

### Polarization

When a dielectric is exposed to an external field, it creates dipoles, with a reverse field caused by polarization to counteract the external field.

>[!example] Polarization
>
>The **polarization** is the sum of the dipoles of $N$ atoms in unit volume:
>
>$$\vec P=\frac 1 V \sum_i^N \vec {p_i}$$
>

For small atomic displacements, we get $\vec p =\alpha · \vec E$ , with [[Atomic polarizability]] $\alpha$. 

Depending on the material's molecular structure, the polarization occurs in these main ways:
- Electronic polarization: The electron cloud of an atom shifts slightly away from the positively charged nucleus.
- Ionic polarization: In ionic compounds, positive ions and negative ions are pulled in opposite directions by the electric field.
- Orientation (dipolar) polarization: In molecules that already possess a "permanent" dipole (like water), the electric field. physically rotates the entire molecule to align with the field.
- Interfacial (space charge) polarization: Free charges accumulate at the boundary layers or defects of multi-phase materials.

### Forces and electric field energy in dielectrics

In a uniform electric field, the net force on a dielectric is zero (opposite forces perfectly cancel).

In a non-uniform field, the dielectric is subjected to a net attractive force that pulls it to the direction of the increasing field strength.

Their surface charge density is equal to the polarization:

$$\sigma_{pol}=\frac {Q_{pol}}A =\frac {N·q·d·A}A =P$$

The field inside the dielectric becomes lower due to the relative permittivity, then we introduce the dielectric susceptibility $\chi =N· \frac \alpha {\epsilon_0}$ , then we can describe the dielectric's energy with:

$$\vec E_{diel}= \frac{\sigma_{free}-\sigma_{pol}}{\epsilon_0}\hat r= \vec E_{vac}-\frac P{\epsilon_0}$$


$$ \Rightarrow P=\epsilon_0 \chi \vec E_{diel}=\epsilon_0 (\vec E_{vac}-\vec E_{diel}) \qquad \text{and } \qquad \vec E_{diel}=\frac{\vec E_{vac}}{1+\chi}=\frac 1 {\epsilon_r} \vec E_{vac}$$

>[!abstract]- Work in a battery
>
> We get a system with a dielectric into an isolated charged capacitor. Work has to be done to remove the dielectric from the capacitor.
> 
> ![[Pasted image 20260713015936.png|150]]
> 
> The battery supplies charges (hence, energy) into the system. Then the work to remove the battery is:
> 
> $$ W_{bat}=\Delta Q· U$$
> 
> Half of this work is used to increase the field energy and the other half is transformed into $E_{kin}$ for the dielectric.
